Extrapolating Results from Temperature Increase in Southwestern Tajikistan to Kunduz Province in Northeastern Afghanistan¶

For this project, I wanted to perform a climate change analysis on Northeastern Afghanistan using data from National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI). The first step that I took was to look for daily summaries stations in Afghanistan. There are less than a handful of stations in the country, and all of them have very low data coverage -- for instance the data coverage of the station in the capital, Kabul, is 58%.

To circumvent this issue, I looked at the stations in the neighboring Central Asian countries. Since my focus was on Northeastern Afghanistan, I checked the station in Dushanbe, Tajikistan, given that Tajikistan not only borders Northeastern Afghanistan but both, across the boundary, also share a similar geography, as the former and the latter sit on adjoining mountain ranges-- Pamir and Hindukush. Fortunately, the data coverage of the Dushanbe station is very high (97%), which meant that I could perform my analysis on the Daily Summaries dataset from the Dushanbe station and extrapolate the conclusions to Northeastern Afghanistan.

Resampling¶

I decide to resample the data to an annual frequency, because as we can see in the plot above, the temperature fluctuates in a sinusoidal pattern throughout the year, which makes sense since Tajikistan has all four seasons (CountryReports, 2026). So, in order to determine a pattern in long-term climate change, I concluded that resampling the data on a yearly basis, taking average for every year, will resolve the factor of seasonal temperature fluctation and allow us to see the trends of change in climate more clearly.

In [11]:
### resample data to ann. freq. with mean
dush_ann_temp_df = (

    ### resample df
    dushanbe_temp_units_df

    ### sample annually
    .resample('YS')

    ### take mean
    .mean()
)

Linear Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) Regression¶

I decided to use Linear Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) Regression after considering the following assumptions:

  • Random Error: With the exception of the above-mentioned data anomaly in 1999, our data seems to be consistent.

  • Normally Distributed Data: Since we are looking at annual mean temperature, the data is more or less normally distributed, adding support to our choice.

  • Linearity: Since Tajikstan (and Northeastern Afghanistan by extension) is in mid-latitudes and not subject to extreme temperature swings, we can assume linearity in its temperature trends.

  • Stationarity: The data, with only the above-mentioned exception, does not see extreme year-to-year variability, making it mostly align with the stationarity assumption.

### fit an OLS Linear Regression to the data for both time ranges

for df in [dush_ann_temp_df, dush_temp_df_1972_2022]:

    ### define independent variable
    ### reshape 'year' to be a 2D array for scikit-learn
    ind_variable = df.index.year.values.reshape(-1, 1)

    ### define dependent variable
    dep_variable = df['avg_temp_cel']

    ### make linear regression model
    model = LinearRegression()

    ### fit the model on the data
    model.fit(ind_variable, dep_variable)

    ### calculate and print metrics
    print(f'Slope: {model.coef_[0]} degrees per year between {ind_variable[0][0]}-2022')
    print(f'Slope: {model.coef_[0] * 10} degrees per decade between {ind_variable[0][0]}-2022')

Trend of Rising Recorded Temperature Substantiates the On-the-Ground Climate Change Effects Felt in Tajikistan and Afghanistan¶

As indicated in our model, annual mean recorded temperature in Dushanbe, Tajikistan has been increasing between 1930-2022 at a rate of ~0.0256°C/year and ~0.256°C/decade, with this trend having a sharper slope between 1972-2022 at a rate of ~0.041°C/year and ~0.409°C/decade. The outputs of our model are further supported by the results of the research done by Hu et al. showing a 0.39°C/decade rate of near-surface air temperature increase in Central Asia between 1979-2011 (Hu et al., 2014).

When put in comparison with the rate of increase of global annual temperature since 1850 at 0.06°C/decade and since 1975 at 0.20°C/decade, we see a much higher rate of temperature increase recorded in Dushanbe (NOAA, 2025). However, the Dushanbe rates are similar to the rates of increase of annual temperature in the Asian Continent at 0.18°C/decade between 1910-2024 and at 0.40°C/decade between 1975-2024 (NOAA, 2025).

The trend in the temperature rise fits with the increased frequency, intensity, and duration of heatwaves in Central Asia between 1917-2016, especially in the latter half of this period (Yu et al., 2019). Additionally, according to Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), with continued global warming, Central Asia will be one of the regions that is projected to experience strongest warming of the hot extremes (IPCC, 2022). Some of the major negative impacts of warming temperature in Tajikistan caused by climate change are the initial increase and long-term reduction of water flow due to glacial melting and retreat, destructive floods, desertification, and drought (Republic of Tajikistan - MNP, 2002).

Kunduz is a province located in the western edge of Northeastern Afghanistan sharing similar terrain and climate, as well as a border, with Southwestern Tajikistan. Therefore, the results of our data analysis can be reasonably extrapolated to this province. With fertile lands, Kunduz has a primarily agricultural economy, as more than half of the population's income relies on agriculture (Naval Postgraduate School, n.d.). Therefore, effects of climate change as in floods, such as the one that occured in April 2026 impacting Kunduz, among other provinces in Afghanistan (Afghanistan International, 2026), and especially desertification and droughts will push the population in this province to precarious existential and economic grounds.

This is while we have to consider that poverty, dependence upon natural resources, and lack of awareness have already driven the people in Kunduz to cut trees, leading to further desertification, soil erosion, and increased vulnerability to climate impacts (Ilya, 2026).
